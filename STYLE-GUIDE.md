# STYLE GUIDE
### The Voice Behind the Bryt Ma Library

This guide describes the voice that runs across all fifteen books, so that any chapter — whether drafted today or a year from now, by the author or with help — sounds like it belongs in the same library.

---

## 1. Core Voice Principles

**Plain sentences over impressive ones.** Short, direct sentences do more work than long, ornate ones. If a sentence needs to be read twice to be understood, rewrite it, don't defend it.

**Personal honesty over polish.** The strongest moments across this library (the walk-by in *Cries to Mbale*, the admission of not valuing Agie's friendship) work because they admit something uncomfortable first, before making any larger point. When in doubt, choose the honest sentence over the impressive one.

**Faith-rooted, not preachy.** Several books draw directly on scripture and faith (*Die to Live*, *God of Balimwezo*, *She Da Moon*, *AbleTech*), but the tone throughout is invitational, not instructive. Readers are asked questions more often than they are told answers. Avoid switching into a sermon register — keep the conversational, reflective tone even when the content is spiritual.

**One quotable line per chapter.** Every chapter should produce at least one short, standalone sentence built to be pulled out and shared on its own — this is a deliberate craft choice across the whole library, not just a nice-to-have. Examples already established: *"The hours no one claps for are the ones that make you." / "He didn't need Bobi Wine to be capable. He needed him to be given a door."*

**End with the reader, not with the author.** Nearly every book in this library closes its chapters (or itself) by turning the lens back on the reader with a direct question, rather than a tidy summary. Keep this pattern — it's part of what makes the library feel personal rather than academic.

---

## 2. Sentence-Level Habits

- Favor short paragraphs (2–5 sentences). White space helps a reflective book breathe.
- Use direct address ("you") often, especially at chapter openings and closings — it keeps the tone personal rather than reportorial.
- Avoid jargon and imported business/academic language ("leverage," "optimize," "paradigm") — these clash with the library's plainspoken, testimony-like register.
- Metaphors should be concrete and drawn from ordinary life (the moon and stars, a seed breaking open, a door being opened) rather than abstract or technical.
- Rhetorical questions are used deliberately and sparingly — usually one per chapter, near the opening or closing, not scattered throughout.

---

## 3. Structural Habits Across the Library

- **The three-part chapter rhythm** (used explicitly in *Similar =/= Same*: Surface → Line → Cost) is a good default shape to borrow for other books when a chapter feels shapeless: name the confusion or assumption, name the real distinction or truth, then name the cost of missing it.
- **Case-study chapters** (as in *Kingmaker*, *She Da Moon*) follow: background → the turn/twist → the framing line → the lesson. Keep this shape consistent so readers recognize the rhythm from book to book.
- **Personal-witness chapters** (as in *Cries to Mbale*, *When Agie Left*) follow a near-cinematic opening (a specific moment, present-tense energy even if written in past tense) before widening into reflection. Don't open these chapters with abstraction — open with the scene.

---

## 4. Tone Boundaries — What This Library Does NOT Sound Like

- **Not academic.** No footnote-heavy prose, no hedging qualifiers stacked on every claim. Where a Category A book needs to cite something, do it lightly ("as widely reported at the time") rather than formally.
- **Not political.** Even books that touch political material (*The Chokepoint*, *God of Balimwezo*) stay focused on principle and pattern, never on persuading the reader toward a side. If a sentence reads like it's trying to win an argument rather than teach a lesson, revise it.
- **Not self-help-generic.** Avoid the flattened, motivational-poster register common in a lot of modern self-help writing ("You've got this!" "Believe in your journey!"). The library's warmth comes from specificity and honesty, not encouragement in the abstract.
- **Not sensationalized.** Even emotionally heavy material (the hospital ward in *Cries to Mbale*, the cost of war in *The Chokepoint*) is handled plainly and respectfully — let the facts carry the weight rather than reaching for dramatic language.

---

## 5. A Quick Voice Test
Before finishing any chapter, read it back and ask:
1. Would this sentence survive being read out loud to a friend, or does it sound like it's performing for a reader?
2. Is there at least one line here someone would want to screenshot?
3. Does the chapter end by asking something of the reader, rather than just informing them?
4. If this is a Category A (fact-anchored) book — is every claim here something I could defend if challenged?

If all four hold up, the chapter is in voice.
