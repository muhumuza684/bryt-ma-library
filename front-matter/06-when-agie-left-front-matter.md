## BACK COVER COPY

Agie was my friend before she was my absence. I did not fully understand what our friendship meant while it was in front of me — I took it, as most of us take the things closest to us, mostly for granted. Then she disappeared for two years. And in the space her absence left behind, I finally understood what I had been too close to see.

This book starts with that one friendship, but it does not stay there. It widens into a much bigger question — about peace and war, about health and sickness, about everything we fail to value until it is gone. This is a book about learning to see what's in front of you, before it has to leave to be seen at all.

## INTRODUCTION

I did not respect our friendship the way I should have while Agie was still around.

I am not proud to write that sentence, but I have learned that books like this only work if they start with an honest admission rather than a comfortable one. Agie was there — steady, familiar, easy to take for granted precisely because she was so consistently present — and then, for two years, she was not. She disappeared, in the way people sometimes do, without a clean explanation and without warning.

It was only in her absence that I finally understood the shape of what I had been given. Not because absence is a better teacher than presence — I don't believe that, not really — but because absence forces a kind of attention that presence rarely demands of us. We do not usually study the things that are still standing in front of us. We study, instead, the shape of what's missing.

This book begins with that one friendship, told as honestly as I know how to tell it. But the longer I sat with what happened, the more I noticed the same pattern showing up everywhere else in life, at every scale. Peace works the same way — invisible until war shows you exactly what it cost to keep. Health works the same way — barely noticed until sickness makes you remember every ordinary thing your body used to do without complaint. Even simple presence — a parent, a mentor, a friend who is simply *there* — tends to go unnoticed until it isn't there anymore.

This is, in the end, a book about attention — about learning to actually see the things and people already in your life, instead of waiting for their absence to finally get your attention. It asks a question I am still learning to answer myself: what am I taking for granted right now, today, that I will only fully understand the value of after it's gone?

I hope reading this saves you from having to learn the lesson the same way I did.

## APPENDIX A — A Note on Agie
Details in this book are shared with care and, where appropriate, with permission — this is a story about a real friendship, told honestly, but with respect for the privacy of the person at its center.

## APPENDIX B — The Widened Pattern
This book applies the "presence vs. absence" lens to several other pairs beyond friendship, including peace and war, health and sickness, freedom and captivity, and provision and scarcity. Readers are encouraged to apply the same lens to areas of their own life not directly covered in this book.

## APPENDIX C — Reflection Questions
1. What do you currently have that you suspect you're not fully valuing?
2. Has there been a "return" in your own life — something or someone that came back after being gone — and did it change how you treated it the second time?
