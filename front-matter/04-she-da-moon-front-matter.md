## BACK COVER COPY

There are thousands of stars in the night sky. Bright. Numerous. Easy to notice, easy to forget. But there is only one moon — and it is the moon, not the stars, that people actually navigate by.

*She Da Moon* is built on a simple, old idea dressed in a new image: that a man was never designed to be sustained by many, but by one — a truth as old as Eden, where Adam was given a single Eve, not several. Through the real stories of four couples — spanning Uganda, China, and India, spanning both sides of a political divide — this book shows what it actually looks like when a woman becomes not one of many admirers, but the one light a man's whole life orients around.

This is not a book about celebrity. It is a book about devotion, respect, and what it costs — and what it builds — when a man chooses one moon over many stars.

## INTRODUCTION

Look up at the night sky and count what you see. Thousands of stars, scattered without pattern, indifferent to whether you notice them or not. And then, one moon — fewer in number, but the only light bright enough to actually be useful. Sailors have navigated by it. Farmers have planted by it. It changes the tide of entire oceans, every single night, without fail.

This book asks a simple question, using that image as its guide: **what if the same principle that governs the sky was also meant to govern love?**

Not many stars. One moon.

This is not a new idea — it is one of the oldest ideas there is. In the very first chapter of the human story, a man was given one companion, not several. Not because there weren't other options in creation, but because devotion was never meant to be divided. What this book adds to that old truth is not a new argument, but a set of real, living examples — proof, not just principle.

You will meet four couples in these pages. Two are from Uganda, standing on opposite sides of the country's political divide — proof that this principle has nothing to do with ideology. One is from China, one is from India — proof that this principle has nothing to do with geography either. What all four couples share is not fame, and not even always agreement with each other's world. What they share is this: one marriage, sustained over decades, in which a woman built a complete life of her own — her own causes, her own achievements, her own name — while remaining the one light a man's entire journey was built around.

This book is not asking you to admire these four women from a distance. It is asking a much more direct question, aimed squarely at every man who reads it: **are you building a life around one moon, or are you scattering your attention across stars that were never meant to hold the weight you're asking them to hold?**

And to every woman who has ever wondered whether her devotion is seen — this book is also a mirror, held up on purpose, so you can see clearly what your own light has already been doing, whether or not it's ever been named out loud.

## APPENDIX B — A Note on Political Neutrality
Two of the couples in this book sit on opposing sides of Uganda's political landscape. This is intentional, not incidental — the book's thesis is proven, not weakened, by showing that the same principle of devotion holds true regardless of political affiliation. Readers are encouraged to receive this book as a book about marriage and devotion, not as commentary on any political position.
