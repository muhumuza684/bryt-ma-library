## BACK COVER COPY

Every conflict looks winnable before it starts. Every leader believes their calculation is the exception. This book follows three decision points — a narrow strait where a fifth of the world's oil must pass, a long border where a larger power bet everything on speed, and an island where the hardest decision hasn't been made yet — and asks the one question every leader tends to answer wrong.

Not: *can we win?*

But: *what will winning actually cost?*

No nations are named in this book. No side is taken. What remains, once the names are stripped away, is something more useful than a history lesson — a study of how smart people, holding enormous power, still consistently mistake capability for cost. And how that mistake, repeated at the highest levels, is really no different from the one we all make in smaller rooms, over smaller stakes, every day of our lives.

## INTRODUCTION

There is a narrow strip of water in the world through which a fifth of all oil must pass. Whoever controls it can, in theory, choke the global economy. Whoever threatens it risks turning the world against them. It is, in every sense, a chokepoint — a place where there is no room for a wrong decision, because the margin for error has already been used up by geography itself.

This book borrows that image as its central metaphor, but it is not really a book about a strait, or about any specific country, war, or conflict. Every actor in these pages is anonymized on purpose. No nation is named. The word "war" does not appear in these pages as a label — only as something that happens when a decision goes wrong. What you will read instead are three decision studies, each built from real, documented patterns of how modern conflicts actually unfold — stripped down to their bare mechanics, so that what remains is not politics, but *decision-making under pressure.*

Why strip the names away? Because names invite sides. And the moment a reader picks a side, they stop learning the actual lesson — which has nothing to do with who was right, and everything to do with a mistake that keeps repeating itself at every level of human decision-making, from war rooms to boardrooms to kitchen tables: **the confusion of capability with cost.**

Every actor you'll meet in these three studies had the capability to act. What none of them fully priced in, going in, was the true cost of acting — in time, in resources, in the will of the people they underestimated. This is not a flaw unique to any one country or ideology. It is a flaw in how humans forecast difficulty when they are the ones holding the power.

Each of the three scenarios in this book ends at a different stage. One ends in a fragile, unresolved ceasefire. One is still, at the time of writing, grinding on years past its original timeline. One has not happened yet at all — and that scenario is included deliberately, so you can watch a decision being weighed in real time, rather than only read about one after the fact.

You are not asked to take a side in this book. You are asked to sit in the seat of the decision-maker, at each fork in the road, and ask yourself honestly: *what would I have decided — and would I have been right about what it would cost?*

## APPENDIX A — A Note on Anonymization
The scenarios in this book are built from real, publicly documented patterns of conflict. Countries, leaders, and specific incidents are deliberately not named, and the word "war" is avoided as a label throughout. This is a deliberate editorial choice, not an attempt to obscure or mislead — the intent is to keep the reader's focus on the decision-making pattern itself rather than on any political position.

## APPENDIX B — Discussion Questions (per scenario)
1. At the first decision point, what would you have chosen, and why?
2. Where did the actors mistake capability for cost?
3. What would "success," honestly defined, have looked like at the outset — and did it match what actually happened?

## APPENDIX C — Suggested Use
This book works well for individual reading, but was also built with group discussion in mind — churches, classrooms, and leadership teams may find it useful as a short case-study series for teaching decision-making under pressure in contexts far removed from geopolitics.
