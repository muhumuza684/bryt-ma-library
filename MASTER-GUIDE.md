# MASTER GUIDE
### How to Research, Write, and Improve Every Book in This Library

This guide applies across all ten titles. Read it once, fully, before starting work on any individual book — then return to the relevant section whenever you're stuck on research, accuracy, or direction.

---

## 1. The Two Kinds of Books in This Library

Every title in this library falls into one of two categories, and knowing which one you're working on changes how you should research and write it.

**Category A — Fact-Anchored Books** (must be rigorously researched)
*Similar =/= Same, The Chokepoint, God of Balimwezo (all 3 volumes), She Da Moon*

These books use real events, real people, or real documented patterns as their proof. Their entire credibility depends on accuracy. A single wrong detail — a misquoted fact, a wrong date, a fabricated claim attributed to a real person — can undo the trust built by everything else in the book. Treat every factual claim in these books as something that must survive a skeptical reader fact-checking it in real time.

**Category B — Voice-Anchored Books** (must be rigorously honest)
*Cries to Mbale, When Agie Left, Retries, Die to Live, AbleTech, Blind on Purpose*

These books draw their power from personal testimony, composite examples, or timeless principle rather than documented current events. Their credibility depends less on external fact-checking and more on emotional honesty and internal consistency. The research task here is smaller, but the honesty bar is higher — readers can tell when a personal story has been exaggerated or sanitized.

---

## 2. Research Rules for Category A Books

**Before writing any chapter that mentions a real person, event, or statistic:**
1. Search for the claim directly — don't rely on memory or assumption, even if it "sounds right."
2. Confirm the claim from more than one independent source where possible, especially for anything contested or recent.
3. Note the date the information was current as of — fast-moving situations (politics, ongoing conflicts, current officeholders) can change between your research and publication.
4. If a claim can't be verified, either cut it or clearly flag it as unverified — never publish a plausible-sounding guess as fact.

**Specific guidance per book:**

- ***Similar =/= Same*** — legal/illegal pairs vary by country. Always specify jurisdiction when a legal distinction is central to a pair (e.g., "in most countries," "under [type of] law"), rather than presenting one country's law as universal.

- ***The Chokepoint*** — this book depends entirely on its anonymization holding up. Every fact used to build a scenario should be double-checked against real reporting, then stripped of identifying names/dates before writing. Re-read each finished chapter and ask: could a reader identify the real country or conflict from context clues I didn't intend to include? If yes, revise.

- ***God of Balimwezo* (all 3 volumes)** — these involve real, living political figures. Every claim about who did what, when, and why must be sourced from public record (election results, official statements, credible news reporting) — never from assumption or rumor. Avoid attributing private thoughts, motives, or quotes to real people unless those are things they've said publicly. Re-verify facts close to publication, since political situations and officeholders can change.

- ***She Da Moon*** — same rule as above for all four featured couples. Do not include any claim about a real person's relationship, marriage, or personal life that isn't publicly documented. If a story sounds too good, dramatic, or convenient to be true (as one earlier candidate example turned out to be), verify it especially hard before using it — see Appendix C below.

---

## 3. Guidance for Category B Books

These books don't need the same intensity of fact-checking, but they need a different kind of discipline:

- ***Cries to Mbale, When Agie Left*** — these are personal-witness books. The research task is mostly internal: sit with the memory long enough to recall it honestly, including the parts that don't flatter you. Where real, living people are named (as in the gratitude sections), keep their portrayal accurate to how the events actually happened, and consider whether they'd be comfortable being named this way.

- ***Retries*** — since celebrity examples were deliberately removed, resist the temptation to reintroduce a real, named person's story without their consent. Composite stories should still feel emotionally true — draw on real patterns you've witnessed or heard about, generalized enough to protect any one individual's privacy.

- ***Die to Live*** — verify any scripture references cited for accuracy of translation and context before publishing; a misquoted or out-of-context verse undermines a book that leans on scriptural authority.

- ***AbleTech*** — composite case studies should be built from real patterns (things you've observed, heard, or researched about disability and achievement broadly) but never modeled closely enough on one identifiable real person that they could be recognized without consent. Disclose in the book's front matter that case studies are composite/illustrative.

- ***Blind on Purpose*** — this book leans on proverbial and wisdom-tradition material. Where you draw on a specific cultural or religious proverb, note its origin honestly rather than presenting borrowed wisdom as if it were newly coined.

## 4. What to Always Add

- **A single, quotable line per chapter.** Every chapter across every book should produce at least one short, standalone sentence that could work as a social media caption or pull-quote. This is what actually spreads a book's ideas beyond its readers.
- **A concrete "so what" at the end of every chapter.** Don't let a chapter end on reflection alone — give the reader something to do, notice, or ask themselves.
- **Sourcing transparency for Category A books.** Where a book makes a factual claim readers might want to verify themselves, a lightly-noted source (even informally, e.g. "as widely reported at the time") builds trust rather than undermining the narrative flow.
- **Consistency checks across the whole library.** Several books share people, places, and themes (e.g., Doreen, Agie, and the matron appear in more than one book; Bobi Wine appears in three separate projects). Before publishing, check that shared details are described consistently across every book they appear in.

## 5. What to Always Avoid

- **Never invent a quote and attribute it to a real, named person.** If you don't have a verified public statement, paraphrase the idea without quotation marks, or don't attribute it to them at all.
- **Never present a single anecdote as proof of a universal pattern** without acknowledging it's one example among many possible ones — this is especially important in *Retries* and *AbleTech*, where the whole point is that the pattern is common, not rare.
- **Never let a Category A book's political material tip into taking a side.** Where a book (especially *The Chokepoint* or the *God of Balimwezo* series) touches on political territory, keep the focus on the pattern or principle being taught, not on persuading the reader toward a political position.
- **Never reuse a real disabled person's specific, identifiable story in *AbleTech* without their explicit consent** — composite examples exist specifically to avoid this problem; don't quietly reintroduce it later.
- **Never let personal-witness books (Category B) drift into unverifiable claims about other real people** — a personal memory of your own experience is yours to tell; a claim about what someone else was thinking or intending is not, unless they told you directly and you're comfortable attributing it to them.

## 6. A Note on International Reach

Several books in this library (*Similar =/= Same*, *The Chokepoint*, *Die to Live*, *Blind on Purpose*) were built specifically with international audiences in mind. When revising these:
- Prefer universal, culturally-portable examples over hyper-local ones as the spine of the book; local examples can supplement, not anchor.
- Keep chapters short (1,500–3,000 words) — this format translates more cleanly and excerpts better for social sharing than long-form chapters.
- Avoid idioms or references that require specific cultural context to land; where local flavor is intentional (as in *Cries to Mbale* or the *Balimwezo* series), lean into it rather than diluting it — those books are meant to read as rooted, not global.

## 7. Before You Publish — A Final Checklist
- [ ] Every factual claim in a Category A book has been independently verified within the last month before publication
- [ ] No real person is quoted saying something they didn't actually say
- [ ] No real, living disabled person's private story appears without consent (AbleTech)
- [ ] Political material is framed around principle, not persuasion
- [ ] Every chapter has at least one quotable, shareable line
- [ ] Shared people/places across multiple books are described consistently
- [ ] The book's front matter (title, subtitle, introduction) matches what's actually inside — revise the front matter if the manuscript has evolved since it was written
