# Cries to Mbale

## Concept
A personal-witness nonfiction book built on a real encounter: the author nearly walked past an old lady near Mbale who was carrying a baby with a bandage over her eyes, but felt compelled to turn back and greet them. That moment led to a friendship, the discovery that the child had cancer and needed an eye removed, and a visit to a hospital ward with more than 30 children affected by similar conditions. The book's mission: encourage churches to help vulnerable people the way mission hospitals do — modeled explicitly on Mbarara Ruharo Mission Hospital — arguing that if every church did the same, far more people would receive help.

## The Real Engine of the Book
The turning point is the near-miss: the author almost walked past. Something compelled him to stop. That moment (nearly missing someone, then being moved to act) is universally relatable, whether or not a reader has ever been near a mission hospital — worth opening the book with, cinematically: the walk-by, the pull to turn back, the greeting, the bandage.

## Drafted Structure

1. **The walk-by and the pull to turn back** — the opening, cinematic
2. **Meeting them, the soda, becoming friends** — the human connection before the full weight of the situation is known
3. **What was learned about the child's condition, and what the hospital revealed**
4. **The ward** — more than 30 children in the same situation; this is the gut-punch that turns a personal encounter into a call to action; the number does more work than any argument could
5. **Why mission hospitals like Ruharo matter, and what happens if every church did the same** — the concrete, replicable answer, not just an appeal to awareness
6. **A direct call to the reader** — what one person's stopping can multiply into

## Closing Chapter / Dedication — Gratitude Section
The book closes (or opens, as a dedication) by naming real people who showed the author kindness — moving the book's spirit from "here's what compelled me to act" to "here's proof this spirit already exists all around us, quietly, in ordinary people, not just institutions." Tone here should be pure gratitude, not argument or case-study analysis — just naming people plainly and letting their kindness speak.

**People to thank:**
- **Mrs. Muhumuza** — his landlady in Mbarara
- **Mzee Babara** (Kibaale) — gave his mother free rental space for her shop for 10 years, asked nothing in return
- **Hajat Mama Mzungu** (Mbarara) — gave him free meals
- **Hajat Madina** (Mubende) — gave his mother business loans
- **Abooki Teddy** — treated the entire village with herbal medicine
- **His junior school matron** — taught him to pray, taught him English, taught him self-care
- **Agie** — his best friend from school
- **Doreen** — shared food with him in hostel, paid for something he needed
- **Dr. and Mrs. Kasangaki Atwooki** — cared for him during a difficult time in his life

**Suggested treatment:** Use both a short dedication page up front (to set the tone) AND a fuller closing chapter (to give the stories room to breathe) — e.g., "To Mrs. Muhumuza in Mbarara, who gave me more than a room to rent. To Mzee Babara in Kibaale, who gave my mother ten years of free space for her shop and asked for nothing back. Kindness like yours is the quiet architecture of a better world." The closing chapter can expand each name into a few paragraphs, showing HOW their kindness worked practically over time — years of small, sustained decisions rather than one dramatic act — mirroring the mission hospital point: it's not one grand gesture, it's showing up for someone again and again.

**Design intent:** ending on people who are still right there, still doing good, keeps the book from ending too heavy after the hospital ward chapter.

## Tone
Personal witness, first-person, unembellished — this is not a research-driven or case-study book like the others; its power comes from direct, honest testimony.
