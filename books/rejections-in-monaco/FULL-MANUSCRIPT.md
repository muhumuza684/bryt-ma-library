# REJECTIONS IN MONACO
## *Eddie Mutwe: Unknown*

### MASTER READING EDITION

# Chapter 1: The Boy With Three Shirts

Mbarara was still dark when Eddie washed the only shirt he trusted himself to wear twice in one week. The basin stood beside a narrow room where the morning cold came through the window before the sun did. He squeezed the shirt, hung it over a line, and looked at the other two.

Three shirts.

That was the wardrobe.

At university, three shirts were not enough to disappear into the crowd. They became a quiet announcement. A man could say nothing about money and still be understood.

Mbarara University of Science and Technology had its own rhythm. Students crossed the campus with books under their arms, phones in their hands, jokes already moving between them. Eddie walked differently. He watched first.

He was studying Social Sciences. Rich Elwelu was farther ahead in Computer Science, a Year 3 finalist whose future seemed to have a road already painted on it. Rich arrived with the confidence of someone who did not have to calculate every small expense.

Acheng moved through campus as if the place had been built to receive her. She dressed well. She knew people. She could turn a simple walk into an entrance.

Eddie had words.

For a while, he believed words were enough.

He watched Acheng from a distance before he ever approached her. He noticed the way she laughed, the way she checked her phone, the way she never seemed to hurry.

He did not know yet that the distance between two people could be measured in more than metres.

> **Pull quote:** Three shirts can tell a whole story before a person says a word.


# Chapter 2: The Answer Acheng Gave Him

Eddie chose a quiet place to speak to Acheng because he had rehearsed the conversation too many times to risk an audience.

He told her he liked her.

The sentence sounded smaller after it left his mouth.

Acheng looked at him for a long moment. She did not laugh. That made the answer harder.

She asked what he wanted her to say.

Eddie said he wanted honesty.

The honesty came slowly.

She spoke about the life she imagined, about the things she noticed, about how different they seemed. She did not need to insult his clothes. She only looked at them. She did not need to insult his course. The comparison with Rich already existed around them.

Eddie understood more from the pauses than the words.

Rich had a car. Eddie had bus fare.

Rich looked like a man already arriving somewhere. Eddie looked like someone still asking the road for directions.

When Eddie walked away, Mbarara had not changed. Students still laughed. Motorcycles still passed beyond the campus. Someone was selling food nearby.

Only Eddie had changed.

He discovered that rejection could be quiet.

It did not always slam a door.

Sometimes it simply left the door open for someone else.

> **Pull quote:** Some doors close without making a sound.


# Chapter 3: The Scholarship

The scholarship notice did not look like a miracle.

It looked like a notice.

There was an application process, documents, deadlines, academic requirements, and a destination that sounded impossibly far away.

China.

The Chinese Government Scholarship had a history in Uganda going back decades. For qualifying students, scholarship packages could cover major study costs and international airfare. That mattered to Eddie because the hardest part of dreaming was not always the dream itself. Sometimes it was the cost of reaching the first airport.

He found the forms.

He read them more than once.

He collected documents.

He asked questions.

He waited.

Waiting became another kind of test. Every email felt important. Every silence felt like rejection returning in a different uniform.

Then the answer came.

He had qualified.

For several minutes he did not tell anyone.

He sat with the message open on his phone.

China was no longer a country in a documentary. It was a destination.

He began imagining what he would need.

Clothes.

Shoes.

Luggage.

Money.

The list was longer than his resources.

But for the first time in a long time, the future had given him something more useful than sympathy.

It had given him a direction.

> **Pull quote:** A direction can be worth more than sympathy.


# Chapter 4: The Sweater

At home, Eddie's packing took less time than the people around him expected.

There was not much to pack.

He folded what he had.

The sweater mattered most.

It was not expensive. It was not special. It simply belonged to him, and it had survived the years before the scholarship.

At Entebbe International Airport, the departure board made the journey feel real.

The airport was bright in the way airports are bright: too clean for the hour, too awake for the people moving through it.

Eddie held his passport carefully.

He had imagined leaving Uganda many times. He had not imagined how strange it would feel to actually leave.

At the gate, other passengers had larger bags.

Eddie looked at his.

Small.

Light.

Almost embarrassing.

Then he remembered why he was going.

The plane lifted over the darkness.

Uganda became lights beneath a cloud.

Eddie pressed his forehead toward the window.

He did not know that a country could disappear so quickly.

> **Pull quote:** Leaving home is an event the body understands before the mind does.


# Chapter 5: The Other Side of the World

China met Eddie with distance before it met him with opportunity.

The airport announcements seemed to arrive in a language that had no relationship with the sounds in his head. Signs carried characters he could not read. People moved quickly.

Then came the weather.

The cold entered his body through places he had never thought to protect.

His sweater suddenly became important.

Campus life was another adjustment. The dormitory was not Uganda. Food was not Uganda. Even the silence between strangers felt different.

Eddie learned small survival routines.

How to find the right building.

How to use transport.

How to buy food without having the right words.

How to watch what other students did before trying it himself.

At night he called home.

The call was short because he did not want anyone to hear the loneliness in his voice.

He said he was fine.

Then he ended the call and stared at the ceiling.

The scholarship had carried him across the world.

It had not carried his mind for him.

That part of the journey had to be done alone.

> **Pull quote:** A scholarship can carry a person across the world without carrying away loneliness.


# Chapter 6: The Name They Could Not Pronounce

The first time a lecturer asked Eddie a question in class, he knew the answer.

He also knew he might not be able to say it quickly enough.

Language had become a wall.

Not an impossible wall.

A slow wall.

Eddie began preparing before class. He wrote down terms. He listened to recordings. He repeated sounds until his mouth stopped fighting them.

His first academic embarrassment was ordinary.

He misunderstood an instruction.

He submitted something that did not answer the question the way the lecturer expected.

He received feedback.

For a few minutes he wanted to disappear.

Then he read the feedback again.

The paper had not rejected him as a person.

It had told him where he was weak.

That distinction changed him.

He stopped treating every correction as humiliation.

He started treating it as information.

The boy who had been rejected by Acheng had carried that rejection like a verdict.

China slowly taught him to carry failure like a map.

> **Pull quote:** Correction becomes useful when humiliation stops being the goal.


# Chapter 7: The Price of Being Poor

The scholarship paid for things Eddie could not have paid for alone.

But there were still small expenses.

A better jacket.

A phone repair.

Food outside the basic routine.

A trip with classmates.

A gift for someone.

A train ticket.

The scholarship had changed his access to education. It had not turned him into a rich man.

Eddie watched classmates spend without thinking.

He learned to calculate.

Sometimes he stayed behind.

Sometimes he invented reasons.

Sometimes he simply said no.

At first, saying no made him feel poor.

Later it made him feel disciplined.

There was another kind of poverty too: the poverty of not knowing what people were talking about.

He had entered a world with customs he did not understand.

He learned by watching.

He learned which questions were rude.

Which questions were normal.

When to speak.

When to wait.

How to accept food.

How to refuse politely.

How to be a foreigner without apologising for existing.

> **Pull quote:** Being funded is not the same thing as feeling rich.


# Chapter 8: A Door With No Handle

The door did not have a sign saying NO.

That was what made it difficult.

Eddie wanted an opportunity outside the classroom. He asked around. He sent messages. He introduced himself.

Some people answered.

Some did not.

One opportunity required experience he did not yet have.

Another depended on language.

Another depended on a network Eddie had not built.

He began noticing a pattern.

A closed door was not always an insult.

Sometimes the person on the other side simply did not know him.

So Eddie changed his strategy.

Instead of asking for the biggest opportunity, he asked for small responsibility.

Instead of saying he was capable, he tried to become useful.

He helped.

He showed up.

He returned messages.

He learned.

Trust came slowly.

It came without applause.

But when it arrived, Eddie understood something he had not understood in Mbarara.

Sometimes the key to a door is not a powerful person.

Sometimes it is a history of being reliable.

> **Pull quote:** Trust is often built through small tasks that nobody celebrates.


# Chapter 9: The First Time Eddie Was Trusted

The first real responsibility Eddie received was not glamorous.

It was a task.

Someone needed a person who would do it correctly and return when it was finished.

Eddie accepted.

He checked the details twice.

He finished.

Then he reported back.

Nobody clapped.

Nobody gave him a trophy.

But the next time there was work, someone called him.

That second call mattered more.

Eddie began to understand how reputation is built.

Not in speeches.

In small repetitions.

He became known as the person who could be trusted with a task.

That reputation travelled farther than he expected.

A classmate introduced him to another person.

That person knew another.

The world was opening through ordinary human connections.

Eddie thought about Acheng sometimes.

But less often.

The strange thing about growth was that the old rejection remained in memory while losing its control over the future.

> **Pull quote:** Reputation grows quietly, then suddenly opens a door.


# Chapter 10: The World Beyond the Campus

The first train journey that carried Eddie far from campus felt like a private education.

Outside the window, China kept changing.

Buildings gathered into cities.

Cities loosened into open land.

Stations appeared and disappeared.

People boarded with bags, children, food, phones, tired faces.

Eddie watched.

The world was not one thing.

China was not one picture.

It was millions of ordinary lives moving at once.

He began travelling when he could.

Sometimes the trip was carefully planned.

Sometimes he missed a connection.

Sometimes he got off at the wrong place and had to work out how to continue.

Those mistakes became stories.

He learned to navigate without panic.

He learned that being lost was often temporary.

He learned that strangers could help without knowing his history.

He also learned that homesickness could appear in beautiful places.

A crowded street could still make him miss Uganda.

A good meal could still remind him of home.

Travel did not erase where Eddie came from.

It made the map larger.

> **Pull quote:** A larger map does not erase the old home; it changes its size.


# Chapter 11: The Man Who Was Still Unknown

There came a point when Eddie stopped checking whether Acheng had noticed his progress.

He had not planned for that moment.

It simply happened.

His attention moved from proving something to building something.

He learned how introductions worked in unfamiliar rooms.

He learned to explain his background without apologising for it.

He learned that the world contained people who had never heard of Mbarara and did not need to.

That was not an insult.

It was freedom.

Eddie had spent years measuring himself through the eyes of people who already knew his history.

Now he was meeting people who knew nothing.

They judged the person standing in front of them.

Sometimes they liked him.

Sometimes they did not.

Sometimes he succeeded.

Sometimes he failed.

For the first time, rejection was no longer connected to one girl's answer.

It had become part of the wider world.

And the wider world was big enough that one closed door could not define the whole journey.

> **Pull quote:** Freedom begins when one person's opinion stops being the measurement of your future.


# Chapter 12: The City Above the Sea

Monaco appeared differently from the Uganda Eddie remembered.

The sea was close.

The hills rose behind the built-up streets.

Port Hercule held boats that looked less like transport and more like floating architecture.

Around Monte-Carlo, polished surfaces, expensive cars, hotels, restaurants, and tightly packed luxury created a visual language Eddie had seen before only through screens.

He walked slowly.

Not because he was lost.

Because he was looking.

Monaco was small.

Its wealth was not.

Eddie thought about the three shirts.

He remembered the sweater.

He remembered the airport.

He remembered the rooms where he had once measured his worth by who was sitting inside.

Here, the question was different.

Who knew you?

Who could introduce you?

Who trusted you?

Who had your name in their phone?

The city did not have to reject him.

It only had to continue without noticing him.

That was enough to make him feel the old distance again.

> **Pull quote:** A city can be beautiful and still make you feel invisible.


# Chapter 13: The Room They Did Not Prepare for Him

The invitation was real.

So was the room.

The problem was that Eddie did not know what the room expected.

His clothes were good now.

His shoes were good.

His passport had taken him across borders.

But confidence in one room did not automatically transfer to another.

A conversation moved around him.

Names appeared that he did not know.

People referred to places he had never visited and institutions he had only read about.

Nobody insulted him.

Nobody told him to leave.

He simply felt the invisible border.

When the evening ended, Eddie walked outside.

The Mediterranean air was cool.

The lights on the hills looked close enough to touch.

He could have spent the night being angry.

Instead he wrote down the names of the people he had met.

He searched the organisations they represented.

He studied the work.

He realised the room had not been the whole world.

It had only been one room.

He did not need to conquer it.

He needed to build something that could stand without it.

> **Pull quote:** One room is never the whole world.


# Chapter 14: What Eddie Built

After Monaco, Eddie stopped chasing appearances.

He still cared about presentation.

But he cared more about substance.

He learned to build relationships slowly.

He learned that money could open some doors but could not manufacture trust.

He learned that competence could travel.

A project completed in one country could lead to a conversation in another.

A person helped today could remember you years later.

A failed negotiation could become knowledge.

A mistake could become a system.

The boy who once counted shirts now counted something else: responsibilities.

Who depended on him?

What had he promised?

What could he deliver?

What would happen if he failed?

Power, Eddie discovered, was not simply having money or being recognised.

Power was the ability to affect what happened next.

That made it more dangerous.

And more important.

> **Pull quote:** Power begins where your decisions start changing what happens to other people.


# Chapter 15: Entebbe Again

When Eddie returned through Entebbe, the airport was familiar in a way that surprised him.

The signs were familiar.

The voices were familiar.

The heat felt immediate.

He watched people move with the confidence of people who belonged.

He belonged too.

But he had changed.

Outside, Uganda received him with traffic, phone calls, plans, requests, and people who suddenly wanted meetings.

The small bag was no longer the symbol of poverty it had once been.

It was simply a bag.

Eddie looked at his reflection in a dark airport window.

For a second he saw the student with three shirts.

Then a phone rang.

The present returned.

> **Pull quote:** Coming home can reveal how far you have travelled.


# Chapter 16: The Office at the Top of the Hill

The fictional future office stood above a city that had taught Eddie how quickly status could change.

By then, people called him important.

He did not always like the word.

The governor's office in this story is an imagined future political structure, not a claim about Uganda's present constitutional arrangement.

The work was less glamorous than the title.

Budgets.

Roads.

Hospitals.

Staff.

Complaints.

Competing interests.

People asking for favours.

People accusing him of ignoring them.

Every decision created another person who thought he had decided wrongly.

Eddie discovered a new kind of rejection.

It came from people who had supported him and now disagreed with him.

It came from people who wanted something he could not give.

It came from people who believed power had made him unreachable.

He remembered the boy who had once stood outside rooms.

Now he was the person holding the schedule.

That frightened him more than he admitted.

> **Pull quote:** The title is easier to carry than the responsibility beneath it.


# Chapter 17: Janet

Janet arrived in Eddie's life through a file.

Her name was typed near the top.

The request was formal.

The problem was urgent.

Then Eddie saw the family details.

Acheng.

Rich.

For a moment the office disappeared.

He was back in Mbarara.

Three shirts.

A quiet conversation.

The answer.

The car.

The feeling of being the poorer man in a room.

Now the people from that room were asking for help.

Janet had done nothing.

That was the first thing Eddie understood.

Whatever had happened between adults years ago did not belong to her.

He read the medical request again.

He asked what was needed.

He asked what could legally be done.

He asked which institution had authority.

The staff waited.

Power had brought the past back to his desk.

Now he had to decide what to do with it.

> **Pull quote:** The innocent should not pay for the history of adults.


# Chapter 18: The Stamp

The stamp sat on the desk.

It was a small object.

A stamp.

A signature.

A decision.

Eddie could have delayed the file.

He could have made the family feel what he once felt.

He could have used procedure as revenge.

Nobody in the room would have called it revenge if he used official language.

But Eddie knew.

He remembered the airport.

The sweater.

The first classroom.

The wrong answer.

The first person who trusted him.

The room in Monaco.

He picked up the file.

He did not approve something because Acheng had once rejected him.

He did not refuse something because Rich had once seemed more successful.

He followed the lawful process.

Where support was available, he made sure the family could access it.

Where his office lacked authority, he directed the request to the institution that had it.

Janet received help.

The story did not end with Eddie humiliating the people who had once humiliated him.

It ended with him refusing to become the room that had once made him feel small.

Outside, the city continued.

Somewhere far away, Monaco was still shining above the sea.

Eddie no longer needed the room to prove that he belonged.

He had finally learned that belonging was not something another person could stamp into your passport.

It was something you carried.

> **Pull quote:** The final victory was not entering the room. It was choosing what kind of man would stand inside it.
